# UX Foundations

## Why this matters

UX is the practice of helping people accomplish meaningful goals effectively, efficiently, and with confidence. Visual polish matters, but a beautiful screen that blocks a user from completing a task is not a successful experience.

## Core concepts

- **User-centered design:** Start with user goals, context, and constraints rather than a requested feature.
- **Usability:** Users can learn, complete, and recover from tasks without unnecessary friction.
- **Mental models:** Design should align with what users expect based on prior experience.
- **Cognitive load:** Limit the amount of information and choices users must hold in mind.
- **Feedback and visibility:** The system should clearly show its status and the result of a user's action.
- **Error prevention and recovery:** Prevent likely mistakes, explain problems clearly, and make recovery easy.

## Think through

For a feature request, ask:

1. Who is the user and what are they trying to accomplish?
2. What does success look like from the user's perspective?
3. What information, decisions, or actions create friction?
4. What can go wrong, and how should the experience recover?
5. How will you know the experience is better?

## Practice

Pick a workflow you use often. Write its primary user goal, map its steps, and identify three moments of unnecessary cognitive load. Sketch one improvement for each.

## Deliverable

Create a one-page problem statement containing the user, their goal, context, pain points, constraints, and a measurable success signal.

## Common interview questions

### How do you approach an ambiguous feature request?

I begin by reframing the request as a user and business problem. I clarify the target users, their goals, the current workflow, constraints, and the outcome we need to improve. Then I identify the riskiest assumptions, gather enough evidence to make a decision, and bring product and engineering into the tradeoffs before moving to solution detail.

### How do you balance user needs with business goals?

I make both explicit and look for the overlap. For example, reducing time to complete a task may improve user satisfaction while also lowering support costs. When goals conflict, I describe the impact on users, business measures, technical effort, and risk so stakeholders can make a deliberate decision rather than treating UX as subjective preference.

## Key terms / cheat sheet

- **User goal:** The outcome a person is trying to achieve.
- **Usability heuristic:** A practical principle for identifying usability problems.
- **Cognitive load:** Mental effort required to understand and act.

## Scenario challenge

An internal employee portal has frequent support requests because people cannot find benefits enrollment. Define the user problem, identify assumptions to validate, and propose a first research and design plan.

## Common pitfalls

- Designing around a stakeholder's requested solution instead of the user problem.
- Optimizing a happy path while ignoring errors and recovery.
- Calling a preference a UX decision without evidence or criteria.

## Review checklist

- [ ] The primary user, goal, and context are explicit.
- [ ] The flow provides feedback and a path to recover from errors.
- [ ] Success can be measured for users and the business.

## Portfolio evidence

Include the original problem framing, task flow, key design decisions, and before-and-after usability evidence.

## Flashcards

**Q:** What is the difference between a feature and an outcome?  
**A:** A feature is an output; an outcome is a meaningful change in user or business behavior.

**Q:** Why is cognitive load important?  
**A:** Excessive mental effort slows decisions, increases errors, and makes experiences harder to learn.
